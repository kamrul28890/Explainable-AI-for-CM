The Core Concept: Black-Box vs. White-Box XAI
Professor Abdallah’s work evaluates how to explain the decisions of complex AI models. He categorizes Explainable AI (XAI) into two types:
Black-Box XAI (e.g., SHAP, LIME): These methods do not know the internal architecture of the AI model. They try to explain the model by tweaking the inputs and seeing how the outputs change
. Because foundational VLMs (like GPT-4o) are often closed-source, you will likely start here.
White-Box XAI (e.g., LRP, Integrated Gradients, DeepLift): These methods look directly inside the model's neural network to see exactly which neurons fired
. If you use open-weights VLMs (like Florence-2), Professor Abdallah will likely suggest using these, as his recent research proves they are inherently more reliable and complete than black-box methods
.
Translating His "6 E-XAI Metrics" to Construction VLMs
In both of his provided papers, Professor Abdallah evaluates XAI methods based on six specific metrics
. If you want to work with him, your project must revolve around testing your VLMs against these exact six metrics. Here is what they mean for your construction project:
1. Descriptive Accuracy
His Definition: Measures how much the AI model's accuracy drops when the "most important" features identified by the XAI are removed
.
Your Construction Application: If the VLM detects a "Fall Hazard" and the XAI says the pixels showing a missing safety harness are the most important, you blur out or remove those harness pixels. If the VLM still predicts a fall hazard, the XAI explanation was wrong. If the VLM's accuracy drops and it gets confused, it proves the XAI accurately described how the VLM makes decisions.
2. Sparsity
His Definition: Measures how concentrated the AI's focus is. High sparsity means the AI only relies on a very small number of features to make a decision
.
Your Construction Application: Construction sites are visually chaotic and cluttered
. You want your VLM to have high sparsity—meaning it ignores the background noise (cranes, dirt, weather) and bases its safety alert strictly on the specific interaction between the worker and the hazard.
3. Stability
His Definition: Checks if the XAI method generates the exact same explanations over multiple identical runs
.
Your Construction Application: If you feed the exact same image of a worker to the VLM three times, does the XAI consistently highlight the exact same visual cues? If the explanation wanders to different parts of the image each time, safety inspectors cannot trust the system.
4. Efficiency
His Definition: The computational time required to generate the XAI explanation
.
Your Construction Application: Your research mentions using agentic VLMs for interactive safety queries via mobile devices or Augmented Reality (AR)
. You need to test if Professor Abdallah's XAI methods can generate hazard explanations fast enough to be deployed on real-time, edge-computing devices on an active site.
5. Robustness
His Definition: The ability of the XAI to resist adversarial attacks or "noise" without generating a false or disguised explanation
.
Your Construction Application: This is critical for the "semantic gap" you wrote about
. You intentionally perturb the images (e.g., add harsh lighting, shadows, or even a sticker on a machine designed to trick the camera). If the VLM is tricked into predicting an unsafe scene is "Safe," does the XAI successfully highlight that the "noise" or "sticker" is what tricked it?
6. Completeness
His Definition: The ability of the XAI to provide a valid, mathematically sound explanation for every possible sample, especially edge cases
.
Your Construction Application: You will take highly complex, corner-case site interactions (e.g., a worker wearing PPE but using equipment improperly). You perturb the visual features until the VLM changes its prediction from "Hazard" to "Safe". If you can drastically alter the image and the VLM's prediction never changes, it proves the model is just guessing and the explanation is invalid.
